Perception

The notion of objects and object distance within or across perceptual modalities create a perceptual space. In the case of visual perception, objects emerge as gradient clusters in color space and distance as the degree of gradation between them. In the case of proprioception, objects exist as point-clouds resistant to force, and distance as the force required to overcome said resistance. In the case of somatosensation, objects exist as textured regions in an adjacency map and distance as the path length between textured regions. In the case of auditory perception, objects exist as grouped frequencies, and distance as the complexity of the transformation from one frequencey group to another. In chemoreception, objects exist as groups of primary properties such as taste, odor and physiological state, and distance as the cardinality of the disjunction of these groups.

These single-modality notions of objects and object distance become more descriptive when composed with other modalities, (e.g. visuo-spatial, visuo-auditory, audio-spatial, etc.). Perception cannot exist in the absence of memory if we conceptualise memory as the container for perception. The most immediate form of perception thus occurs in sensory memory and fades within milliseconds, with the most salient features of sensory perception being rehearsed and thus preserved in working memory across seconds. Working memory acts as an algorithm-generator, rehearsing and processing objects in working memory and writing to long-term memory if this process is not interrupted by the perception of more salient features of the environment (both external and physiological) in sensory memory. Then when subsequent environments are encountered, two systems exist in response, system one which acts instantaneously in response to stimuli based on existing action pathways stored in long-term memory, or sytem two, which acts slowly and deliberately to derive such action pathways to deal with lesser rehearsed stimuli, that move to the automated domain of system one upon rehearsal.

The selective attention test shows two groups of three individuals, one group dressed in white, one in black, and each group passing a ball between themselves as they move around a room. The task is to count the number of passes within the white team. Halfway through the task, an individual in a gorilla suit walks into the middle of the room and waves at the observer, who is blissfully unaware of the gorilla's presence, given their preoccupation with the task at hand. Thus the environment we perceive is a product of the means of interaction we rehearse in response to the perception of that environment. It suggests that the abstract environments one perceives as a part of the rehearsal process of working memory is just as salient and relevant to that which is perceived in immediacy. It frames the knowledge structures constructed from this rehearsal process not as models for that which is perceived but rather models that generate that which is perceived. This is a two-sided coin, and can be taken as empowering, or as claustraphobic. In either case, the search for the 'invisible gorilla' so to speak, is the means by which the individual transforms the way in which the external world is perceived.
