Belief

Let's start with belief in the context of prediction. If I believe that an event \(X\) will occur \(b(X)\) and then perceive that event's occurrence \(p(X)\), the perception of the event's occurrence will reinforce the belief. Similarly, the perception of the event's non-occurrence will reinforce the counter-belief. \[ b(X), \> p(X) \rightarrow b(X) \] \[ b(X), \> p( \neg X) \rightarrow b( \neg X) \] This belief system is empirical in the sense that the belief is constructed directly from observation. Compare this to a system in which it is believed that belief itself affects the perceived occurrence of events. \[ b(b(X) \rightarrow p(X)), \> p(\neg X) \> \rightarrow b(X) \] This belief system is idealist in the sense that observation is constructed directly from belief. If to hold the belief is to see an increase in the occurrence of the event it predicts, the system is idealist and has predictive power.

A hybrid belief system containing contradictory beliefs that are held depending on context may have more predictive power than those same systems in isolation, despite their contradiction. \[ b(b(X) \nrightarrow p(X), \> b(X) \rightarrow p(X)) \rightarrow p(X)\] This stranger system is a sort of intentional cognitive dissonance, where the ability to hold contradictory beliefs is believed to predict the occurrence of the event the belief-holder desires.

In an idealist or dissonant belief system, it seems a habitual loop can be broken by belief in an alternative, more so than struggle towards the alternative or struggle against the habit itself.
