λ-Calculus

The lambda calculus is a formal system for expressing computation. Lambda calculus is powerful in that it defines no primitive data types, instead modelling computation through function abstraction and application alone. The BNF syntax of the lambda calculus is as follows. \[ e ::= \> x \>|\> \lambda x . e \>|\> e \> e \] Thus we have three primitive expressions. We have the variable \(x\) as a lambda term. We have function abstraction \((\lambda x . t )\) where \(t\) is a lambda term, and \(x\) is referred to as the parameter variable. We also have function application \((t \> s)\) where \(t\) and \(s\) are lambda terms.

If a variable is the parameter of any enclosing \(\lambda\) it is said to be bound, else it is said to be free. For example, in the following statement, \(y\) is the only free variable. \[ (\lambda x . ((\lambda z . (x z))(\lambda x . y))) \] We can put this more formally as follows. \[ FV(x) = \{x\} \] \[ FV(M_1 M_2) = FV(M_1) \cup FV(M_2) \] \[ FV(\lambda x . M) = FV(M) \>\backslash\> \{x\} \]

Lambda calculus then formalises a broad notion of term substitution called capture-avoiding substitution. \[ x[x : = N] \equiv N \] \[ x \neq y \rightarrow y[x : = N] \equiv y \] \[ (M_1 M_2)[x := N] \equiv (M_1 [x := N]) (M_2 [x := N]) \] \[ x = y \rightarrow (\lambda y . M)[x := N] \equiv (\lambda x . M) \] \[ y \notin FV(n) \rightarrow (\lambda y . M)[x := N] \equiv (\lambda y . (M[x := N])) \] \[ y \in FV(n) \rightarrow (\lambda y . M)[x := N] \equiv \lambda y' . M [y := y'][x := N] \] With definitions in order we introduce beta-reduction as an expression equivalence to simplify expressions. \[ (\lambda x . t) s \equiv t[x := s] \]
